Turn view — prototype

Running one combat turn currently takes four windows: the tracker for whose turn it is, the stat block for what the creature can do, the Attack Runner to roll it, and the map. This collapses them into one surface. Click a target, then Roll on an action — or type a roll in by hand. Space ends the turn; any initiative pip jumps.

How it works, and what changed in this pass

Every automated path has a manual twin. Enter a to-hit and damage yourself for a roll made with real dice — it goes through the same pipeline, so reactions still apply. The −/+ under the target list changes HP with no attack at all. On a player's turn, hand entry is the surface, since they roll their own.

Reactions fire on someone else's turn, so they are offered at the moment of the hit. Attack Zoey to see Uncanny Dodge halve it; Zindle has Cutting Words, which subtracts a d8 and can turn a hit into a miss. An R beside a name means they hold an unspent reaction that could answer an attack; it refreshes on their turn. The prompt only interrupts when one is actually available, so everything else stays two clicks.

Anyone at the table can react, not just the target. Hit Zoey and you'll be offered her Uncanny Dodge and Defensive Duelist, Zindle's Cutting Words from across the room, and Ulrick's Spirit Shield — which triggers on an ally taking damage, so it could never have appeared while the prompt only asked the victim. Each reactor spends their own reaction. Whether a feature answers your own hit, an ally's, or either is read out of the feature's wording by the extractor.

It knows where everyone is standing. The map sits small in the corner and enlarges when you go to it — it grows as an overlay, so nothing below it jumps, and it stays big while you drag rather than collapsing under your own cursor. Drag a token and the offers change as you do it: Spirit Shield reaches 30 ft, Cutting Words 60 ft, so walking Ulrick into the far corner drops his off the list. Distance is Chebyshev, because 5e counts a diagonal as 5 ft like any other square; straight-line distance would put a creature three squares away diagonally at 21 ft instead of 15 and quietly change who can react. Line of sight is deliberately not checked — the reactions all say "a creature you can see", but whether a wall, a corner or a cloud of fog blocks that is a ruling you make at the table, and a panel that greyed out Spirit Shield because it disagreed with you about a doorway would be worse than one that stays out of the way. Distance is arithmetic and safe to automate; sight isn't.

Yes — the real one would show your actual map. The art here is a synthetic stand-in, drawn rather than borrowed, because dressing this in one of your real maps would flatter the design without testing it. In the panel it would render the battle map's own background and its own token positions — the same relationship it has with the party tracker, one copy of the data rather than a synced duplicate. What the stand-in IS good for is the question worth settling now: whether a token stays readable on top of map art at thumbnail size. Use the map art toggle to compare.

Reactions spend real resources. Cutting Words costs a Bardic Inspiration die, Absorb Elements and Silvery Barbs a spell slot, Spirit Shield needs Ulrick raging — each button shows the pool it will draw from, and greys out when it can't be paid. Spending one empties a pip under the character. Those pools are the party tracker's own shape ({name, type, current, max}), so in the real panel this isn't a copy that needs syncing — it's the same number, and the party card repaints.

Nothing here is typed in. Each creature's reactions are derived from class, subclass, level and feats against a table extracted from your own data/class/*.json — 5 reactions come from base classes, 79 from subclasses, 17 from feats. Zoey has Uncanny Dodge because she is a level-6 rogue. Class and level alone would have found 5 of 101, which is why subclass isn't optional. The chips under each creature show what was derived and where each came from; gold ones are what the prompt will offer. Six have real mechanics — the rest are spent, logged and adjudicated by you, because faking a hundred is worse than admitting to six.

Initiative is editable, and the order follows. The strip was a readout of a number with nowhere to come from. Initiative at the right of the strip turns every value into a field; typing one re-sorts the order immediately, and the turn stays with the creature rather than the slot — drop the Ogre from 18 to 3 mid-turn and it is still the Ogre's turn, now at the bottom. Roll NPCs rolls d20 + modifier for the monsters only, because players call their own out loud; that inverts the rest of the panel, where the automated path is the common one and the manual path is the twin. Ties keep the order the table already settled on instead of shuffling on every keystroke — 5e breaks them by Dexterity or by DM call, and leaving them alone is a DM call. The same mode adds and removes combatants: reinforcements arrive and creatures leave, so an order you can only edit the numbers of is still half a tool. Removing the creature whose turn it is passes the turn on rather than losing it; removing someone above it doesn't move it at all. The Foe / Ally picker is not decoration — side is guessed from character class for the stand-ins, and a summoned wolf has no class, so without it the party's own opportunity attacks would fire at their own wolf. The last combatant can't be removed, because nothing here models a fight with no one in it and there'd be no way back.

Opportunity attacks live on the map. Every other reaction here is triggered by a roll, so it belongs at the moment of the hit — an opportunity attack is triggered by movement, which is why the hit prompt could never house it. Drag Zoey out from beside the Bugbear and the prompt appears; reach is read off each creature's own melee action rather than assumed to be 5 ft, and moving within reach provokes nothing. A creature with a stat block rolls it for real, and one without hands the roll to its player. It goes back through the same pipeline, so the mover can answer an opportunity attack with Uncanny Dodge — one reaction triggering another is correct, not an edge case.

Three smaller decisions taken since the last pass. The result now sits under the Roll buttons rather than at the foot of the panel, because that is where your eye already is; its left edge lights when it holds an outcome rather than an instruction, and the footer took over saying whose turn it is and who is next. The dead space under a two-action monster is now the rest of the stat block — traits, senses, languages — with the log growing into whatever is still left, so the column is full whether the creature is an Ogre or a dragon. And map art defaults to auto: grid when compact, art when enlarged. At 16px a token on plain grid is unambiguous and on dungeon floor it competes with texture for the same pixels. The toggle still pins it on or off, so the comparison that produced that default is there to make rather than just asserted.

  1. Traits are typed in for these stand-ins. Reactions are derived; traits aren't, only because the extractor emits reactions alone. The real panel would read them from the same bestiary entry and data/class/*.json.
  2. The result line and the top of the log now say the same thing. Two views of one event, one detailed and one historical — but worth deciding whether the log should skip the entry it is already sitting under.
  3. A move can only provoke once here, on the drop. Dragging across three squares of a foe's reach in real play is one provoke, so this is right — but a real move tool that animates a path would need to decide that deliberately.
